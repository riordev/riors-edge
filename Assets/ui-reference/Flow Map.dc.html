What the edges say
Four edge types, and the type is the whole argument. Cyan solid replaces the screen and has a back affordance. Violet dashed is a modal over a live world — inventory, Core, doctrines — dismissed with the same key that opened it, never with a Back button. Gold is dialogue-gated: the Forge and the Quartermaster have no tab, no hotkey, and no entry that is not a conversation. Grey dotted is not a transition at all, it is the player walking, and it is drawn because the Anchor's order is enforced by distance rather than by UI.
Why the flow felt bad
Every stop was the same kind of thing — a tab. A tab bar flattens a settlement into a menu, so arriving carried no order and leaving carried no cost. Here the Anchor is a walk with four stops, and each stop is a different kind of edge: stash is a station you use standing at it, the Forge and Quartermaster are people you talk to, Command is a screen at the gate. The player reads the order from the floor plan, and the UI stops being the thing they navigate.
Pause, and what it may not hold
Pause offers exactly four things: inventory, the trees, settings, quit. It may not offer Forge, Quartermaster, stash, Command, or respec — every one of those is a place, and a menu that reaches a place deletes the place. The listen-server question stays open on the map: in HALT the panel is opaque and the world is frozen; in LIVE the panel keeps a 3px orange top rail and the header reads WORLD LIVE, because a menu that lies about whether you can be shot is worse than no menu. Both states are drawn; neither is chosen here.
SHELL LAW
A menu that reaches a place deletes the place.
This is the general rule, and every future request for a shortcut is an instance of it. If a thing is somewhere — the Forge, the Quartermaster, the stash, Command — then reaching it from a menu removes the reason it was somewhere, and the walk that gave the Anchor its order becomes dead space. Test before granting any shortcut: if the new entry point would make the physical location optional, the entry point is the bug. The corollary is the grey edge on this map — grey dotted is not a transition at all, it is the player walking, and it is the only edge type the UI does not own.
The floor plan is a UX constraint
Order enforced by distance makes stash → Forge → Quartermaster → Command → gate a level-design requirement, not a menu one, and whoever authors the Anchor inherits it. The stash sits at the arrival boundary so a full player empties before walking anywhere. Kess's room is deeper in because it is where minutes get spent. The Quartermaster is a counter on the way out. Command is at the gate, because choosing what to run before choosing the build to run it with is backwards. A floor plan that lets a player reach the gate without passing the stash breaks the flow as surely as a pause-menu shortcut would, and no UI change compensates for it.
One comparison surface
Inventory owns the composed-delta view — weapon damage and ability damage as separate rows, effective health against physical — because it is the only surface reachable at the moment an equip decision is made, and the one place the candidate and the equipped piece are already both on screen. The stash keeps triage instead: item level, affix count, and one better-or-worse chip against the equipped piece, which is enough to decide keep or dump across hundreds of rows and cheap enough to compute at that scale. Two comparison surfaces would be two answers to one question, and the stash is the copy that cannot be trusted mid-run, because a run cannot mutate it.
Rulings held open
Four switches at the top move the map instead of resolving it. Class select has four placements and the map opens on the one already on record: end of the prologue, first camp return, roughly level 3, after an induction range where five loaner kits are fired for thirty seconds each. The other three place the choice before a shot has been fired, so the node marks them as contradicting the record rather than as neutral alternatives. Anchors are one settlement or a network. Fast travel has a before and an after, so the map draws both. Each switch changes edges only; no box moves without an edge moving with it.
