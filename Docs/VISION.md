# Rior's Edge

A first-person looter shooter with ARPG progression and MMO social structure,
unfolding a story across many areas. Movement is a core pillar — it is part of
how a character is built, not a fixed utility layer under one. It is not the
thesis. There is no grapple and no tether.

## The premise

An alternate Earth, roughly a century forward. Rifts have opened across the
planet and what comes through is hostile.

They were opened by one person. **Rior** built rift technology as salvation:
reach into an uninhabited timeline, take what a dying biosphere needs, come
back, nobody dies. Humanity used it exactly as designed and then used it more,
until the dead worlds being stripped turned out not to be dead. He concluded
the problem had never been scarcity — he had handed appetite a larger plate —
and that humanity cannot be permitted to reach further than one world. He is
erasing it from timelines, sequentially, working outward.

**This timeline is next. That is what Rior's Edge means: the leading edge of
his advance.** He cannot be reached from inside it. Rifts are the only
direction he can be attacked from, which is why Breakers enter them, and it is
why the game's atomic loop exists at all.

Two families come through. **Vestiges** are rift-native and genuinely alien —
Rior opened the doors, he did not build what came through them. **The Altered**
are refugees from timelines he has already finished, and **severance** — being
cut off from a world that no longer exists — degrades them until nothing is
left but the shape. Severance is reversible inside a working **Anchor**, a
settlement built on rift-suppression hardware. Anchors are the only safe
ground, and ordinary life continues inside them.

And the thing the player does not learn at the start: **closing a rift does not
seal a door. It closes a gap in time, and the timeline behind it never
existed.** The player performs that, hundreds of times, without comment.

## What is true about this game

**Content scales. Players do not.** Area level drives how hard a thing is and
how good its rewards are, and it is never derived from the player. A build that
changes nothing observable is not a build, and player-scaling makes every build
that one.

**Choices beat accumulation.** The level cap is a hard stop with no character
power past it, so the endgame is gear depth rather than a longer bar. Power
moves into decisions — which nodes, which affixes, which rule you break — and
away from how much you have done.

**Identity has four avenues, and class is only one.** How you act, what you
amplify, what you stack, and which rule you break. Class must never be the sole
trunk: a Core-tree build and a gear build each have to be a real answer to
"what is your character". A baseline player is viable understanding none of the
four — that flatness is the comfortable barrier to entry, and innovation is
rewarded on top of it rather than required to reach it.

**Most of a tree is meant to be refused.** A tree a character can finish is a
checklist. A tree that offers far more than any budget can buy is a series of
decisions, and the decisions are the product.

## What it looks like

**Readability beats fidelity.** The player is airborne and moving; silhouette
and value contrast are the whole budget.

**Grounded near-future, degraded outward.** Field-repaired industrial hardware,
recognisable materials, and nature reclaiming the ground it sits on.

**The rift is a colour, and only objects get it.** One narrow reserved band
means rift material reads as foreign with nothing written.

**Wrongness is compositional, not gory.** Vestiges are objects assembled by
something that did not know what a body is for.

## Scope

The first slice proves the loop, not the story: movement, weapons, enemies, a
boss, randomised loot with build-defining items, a tree worth spending in, and
save and resume. **The campaign is post-slice.**
